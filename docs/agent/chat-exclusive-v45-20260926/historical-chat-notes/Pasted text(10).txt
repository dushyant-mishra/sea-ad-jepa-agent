The answer is that hiding the queried gene from the teacher is not necessarily a mistake. But we must give the teacher some other information that the student does not have. The queried gene's measured count: the particular number we do not want the network to reconstruct as its primary objective.

The biological state associated with that gene: a representation of the cell's molecular environment, which may be inferred from other genes and pathways.

Importantly, this state should reflect the actual molecular evidence available in the cell. It should not merely be a learned embedding of the gene's name or a generic representation that barely changes between queries.

2. How is this different from a regular transformer?
A transformer is an architecture. A world model is defined primarily by what it is trained to represent and predict. In fact, JEPA itself can use transformers for both its teacher and student.

Two different learning objectives
Token reconstruction
Predict the missing measurement

Given the remaining RNA and the identity APOE, predict the expression value of APOE. The training target is the measured count or its normalized equivalent.

Our intended JEPA objective
Predict the contextual latent state

Given the remaining RNA and the identity APOE, predict the teacher's multidimensional representation of the molecular state associated with APOE.

The second objective allows the model to learn relationships among molecular programs rather than being rewarded solely for recovering a particular measurement.

However, simply replacing a scalar target with a vector does not guarantee this distinction. A teacher could encode little more than the scalar in a complicated vector, and the student could learn to reconstruct it.

Equally, the teacher could encode only generic microglial activation and produce almost identical targets for many different queried genes.

Neither would satisfy our intended scientific objective.

3. So should we hide the query gene from the teacher?
Here I would refine my previous explanation.

We should not automatically hide the queried gene from the teacher just because we hide it from the student. Doing so may deprive the teacher of valuable evidence about the very state we want it to represent.

There are two scientifically meaningful constructions:

Construction A
Teacher sees richer molecular information, including the query gene.

This potentially gives the teacher a more informative representation of the cell's actual query-associated state. But we must establish that its representation contains meaningful contextual structure beyond the gene's expression value.

Construction B
Teacher sees richer molecular context but not the query's measured value.

The teacher constructs a query-conditioned representation from correlated genes and pathways. This directly limits one form of scalar leakage, but it may omit genuine biological information uniquely carried by the queried gene.

Neither construction is automatically biologically faithful. Both have to be evaluated.

There is also a fundamental limit: if the student observes no information that predicts some part of the teacher's state, no amount of JEPA training can recover that information reliably.

4. What I think your argument changes in our project
The question we should ask is not primarily how can we prevent the teacher from seeing the answer?

It is:

How do we establish that the teacher has constructed a faithful representation of query-associated cellular state, and that the student can predict its biologically meaningful components from the remaining RNA?

That requires three distinct scientific demonstrations:

1. Teacher fidelity: Does the teacher's representation preserve independently measurable aspects of the cell's biology? Does it represent query-associated information rather than merely the queried transcript count?

2. Query specificity: Does the representation meaningfully change when we ask about APOE instead of P2RY12, beyond simply substituting their gene-identity embeddings?

3. Student predictability: Can the student recover the relevant representation from the remaining RNA, and does that performance exceed query-only, generic-cell-state and technical-shortcut controls?

Your underlying point is right: the teacher should be our source of meaningful molecular-state targets, not simply a device for creating vectors. 4. How should the students work together?
I would investigate two possibilities in parallel.

A shared student with specialist heads: One common encoder learns a cellular representation, while separate heads learn core, fine and rare components. This is computationally efficient and lets specialist predictions use information learned by the core encoder.

Multiple specialist students: Different students have their own encoders or partially shared encoders, allowing them to learn different biological structure. This gives rare biology more dedicated capacity, but increases training complexity and makes inconsistencies between students harder to manage.

The scientific experiment should compare these possibilities rather than assume the more complex one will perform better.

Your suggestion that the student team should try to beat the teacher team is also interesting. I would give beat a specific meaning: students should learn to predict the teacher-derived state using less molecular evidence, and their combined representation should eventually be tested for whether it is more reproducible or more informative on independent measurements.

A student cannot reliably reproduce information unique to the teacher's input when that information is absent from its own observations. But its representation might become more useful by discarding unpredictable noise and retaining the shared biological structure.

Teacher–student agreement alone would not demonstrate that improvement.
